What can my character do?

A parade float is moving at ten miles an hour. A woman on the pavement wants to run alongside it and talk to a passenger. Her player can ask a surprisingly precise question in HYBRID. What rating would it take to keep pace? The answer is twenty on one of the game's scales. Give her that rating, and you can play out the conversation while she runs. Give her a rival who wants to reach the passenger first, and you have asked a different question. Knowing two speeds might tell you who arrives first on an empty straight course. It tells you very little about who can persuade the passenger to listen.

That change of question gives a sense of the game. HYBRID is especially interested in what someone of a particular magnitude could do. Its most convincing protagonists are people with a benchmark you can picture and extraordinary characters with a named ability. A fast runner, a low-powered mutant who can change appearance, and a being capable of leaving a lasting creation are all within its reach. It extends the same language to vehicles, planets and cosmic entities. Its ambition is universal, its examples are overwhelmingly at home in superhero fiction, and many of its procedures simulate one narrow feature of a scene. You can bring these people together in a story of your own.

The game master has a documented choice at the outset: the scale appropriate to the campaign. The player can make genuine construction choices, including which advantage to take at a particular stage. In play the text often works backwards from something a known character did to the rating that would explain it. A natural way to run a session, inferred from those examples, is to ask the player for a concrete feat and look together for the rule that models it. The float, the conversation and the order in which people speak are our staging. HYBRID provides the running benchmark.

Read one line of the sheet

Suppose you want to play Kit, an ordinary courier. Write Kit · 16 C2 DP. Kit is ours. Sixteen is the author's example of an ordinary person's rating. Read the three pieces aloud: sixteen, on the C2 scale, in Default Psyche. Keep the number, scale and kind of rating together whenever you copy it. If somebody tells you only that their character has twenty points, you have to ask two more questions before you know how to use them.

Psyche is the broad idea behind the ratings. The author uses it to describe the magnitude of a being. It goes on a runner or a planet and can enter a physical calculation. Reading it as everyday mental health or as a conventional willpower saving throw would make the next line of the sheet quite mysterious. Default Psyche, shortened to DP, is the baseline rating for this particular character. You return to it to find other values. From Kit's sixteen you can determine an aggregate called Average Mind, Body and Soul. Take the square root and you get four. Write “average 4.” You still have the option of describing Kit as physically sturdy or unusually quick-witted. The arithmetic supplies no individual score for either description.

Two further labels let you talk about what the character can sustain or express. Life Span (LS) is a lifespan rating. A separate calculation converts it into years. Power Level (PL) measures the power available in a particular application. At the unmodified starting point in the trade examples, LS and PL coincide with DP. After a trade they can be very different. Record the baseline once and put the altered ratings beside the effect to which they belong. Otherwise a player returning to the sheet next week could easily mistake the cost of sustaining one power for a change to every part of the character.

Consider the author's example of becoming intangible. It begins at 40 C2 DP. The worked choice halves lifespan and doubles power. The sheet for that effect now reads 20 C2 LS and 80 C2 PL, with baseline 40 C2 DP. The player has committed to an exchange. Those twenty lifespan points do not say how many years pass, how many times the power can be used today, or when it switches off. Each of those would need an additional instruction.

When does a bigger number mean a stronger hero?

Kit can hand another player a sheet saying 28.18 C3 DP. That player has the same courier. There is a conversion between the class called C2 and the class called C3, much as two scales on a map can describe the same journey. Here the conversion is nonlinear. Doubling a number in one column will generally do something quite different in the next. The practical reason to convert is that a rule may ask for a character's baseline specifically on C2 or specifically on C3.

The letter C means class in the sense of a magnitude scale. It does not give you an occupation or a list of class features. The regular chain runs from CA to C1 to C2 to C3, with C4 appearing in further comparisons. CA accommodates very large fictional magnitudes with comparatively small numerals. C1 occurs in optional character generation. C2 often supplies the working numbers for characters, lifespan, and movement. C3 appears in comparisons of total points and in rules for particular powers. C0 needs the name of the specific statistic. The author can obtain C0 Psyche in more than one way, and also reports C0 strength and other local measures. There is no general slot for it between CA and C1.

Read across a row to translate one character. Read down the C2 column to compare different baselines. A 16 C2 ordinary person and the 36 C2 mutant from the construction example really do differ. Moving from 36 C2 to the corresponding C3 cell only describes the same mutant a second way. Values in the table are rounded for display. The inverses use the branch appropriate to these ratings above one.

What matters in play is the difference between translating a rating and changing its label while keeping its number. One rule turns a human into a super-mutant by moving the same numeral from C3 DP to C1 DP. Another has a creator retain the numeral but move from C2 DP to C3 DP as the price of making an effect permanent. For the ratings in those examples the first character gains magnitude and the second gives some up. Use the operation the particular rule calls for. An exciting description by itself gives the player no authority to change the class label.

Make someone you can bring to the table

A useful first decision is what you want your character to accomplish on screen. For a courier, you might want to know how fast she can run and whether she can keep a parcel out of sight. Those are two separate requests. An ordinary human baseline can answer the first with a running rule. A mutant creation route can give you a named concealment ability for the second. A single high baseline does not automatically purchase every feat its owner might like to perform.

  1. Agree on the campaign. Tell the GM who the protagonist is and what ordinary means in this story. A campaign about humans among a few mutants can use a different starting magnitude from one about established superhumans. The source gives the GM a choice of scale. The name, personality, occupation and reason to join the others are yours to supply. They can guide the fiction even where there is no numerical field for them.

  2. Choose one of three starting routes. You can take a benchmark, roll a starting value, or build up through advantages. The first two routes finish in this step. For either of them, skip the mutant construction in the next step and go straight to the power entry. For a benchmark character, use a documented example such as 16 C2 DP for the ordinary adult. For a random start, an optional procedure reads the face of a twenty-sided die directly as C1 DP. A roll of 18 in that procedure gives about 37.65 C2 DP. Writing the same roll as 18 C2 would give a very different character. The author also gives pools as alternatives. Agree on the route before seeing the result.

  3. If you chose the third route, build a low-powered mutant from the choices. This route starts at 9 C2 DP. Choose agility from the first list. Its advantage doubles nine to eighteen. Choose increased height from the second list. It adds half the current eighteen, bringing you to twenty-seven. Choose camouflage from the third list. It adds a third of twenty-seven, bringing you to thirty-six. Now write down all three traits and the final baseline. This is one reproducible selection from the author’s menus. The builder lets you choose different entries for each stage. The intermediate numbers are stages in construction, so you do not add them up for a spending budget.

  4. Decide what goes in the power entry. Camouflage on that mutant's sheet tells you that the character possesses it. Elsewhere the author calculates a percentage for phasing or cloaking from a specified total and a base. Treating the mutant's entire 36 as the total for that other rule would be a further choice. You can play the acquisition faithfully while leaving detection range and duration open. Another route sizes a minimum advantage from appearance, abbreviated COM. In its example a starting value of ten gains a full advantage and becomes twenty. This is an alternative worked construction. The text supplies no instruction to apply that increase on top of the three mutant choices.

  5. Apply a limitation or trade only when you can state its terms. Advantages and limitations may recur. The lifespan–power exchange permits either opposite additions and subtractions or opposite multiplications and divisions. Beginning with forty of each, losing twenty lifespan by subtraction can give twenty extra power, finishing at sixty. Halving lifespan can instead double power to eighty. Both roads lead to twenty lifespan. Record the route beside the result, and retain the choice actually specified when reproducing a published example. The rules give precedents for designing powers, with no comprehensive menu setting the price of every disadvantage.

  6. Finish the sheet to the extent the route allows. Calculate average Mind, Body and Soul from C2 DP. The mutant at thirty-six has an average of six. The humanoid write-up asks for name, age, COM, and the amount remaining for powers, with the scale attached. One example uses all its PL for COM and leaves zero for mutant powers. The three-choice mutant build gives no such allocation. Leave that line open and record how the character was made. On the printed sheet an open field means the procedure supplies no value. A zero is a different answer.

The result will feel familiar in some respects. The character has distinguishing traits, an aggregate resembling an attribute, and powers that can carry costs. The source sometimes calls an advantage a skill or talent. It offers an ideal proportion among mental attributes, powers and physical attributes, and uses the word “health” for the physical share. That description alone gives no pool to spend when hurt. Equipment can have ratings of its own, as vehicles do in the rules for size and motion. The edition supplies no common shopping or starting-loadout procedure for these builds. You may write “courier bag” as a fictional possession. Any numerical benefit needs its own support.

Take a turn

Return to the parade. Tell the GM exactly what you want: “I run beside the passenger long enough to speak.” The GM gives the float a speed. Ten miles an hour is a fact established for this scene. The running rule accepts miles per hour and returns the required C2 DP or PL. First take the base-ten logarithm of the speed in mph, add one, then multiply by ten. Ten mph gives twenty. For a new speed of 13 mph, the logarithm is about 1.114, giving a required rating of about 21.14 C2. Going the other way, raise ten to the power of (C2 rating divided by ten, minus one) to get mph. Use this rule for a positive speed. A runner already at twenty meets the float’s ten-mile-an-hour pace. Kit at sixteen falls short under the same model: her corresponding speed is just under four miles an hour. Neither player needs a die to answer the pace question. The conversation itself is still for the people at the table to play.

Change the verb and check the rule again. “I phase through” invokes a power with a different sort of output. With the author's example total of one hundred on C2 and the usual base of ten, the phasing rule yields fifty-five percent. The percentage tells you something about the effect. The passage gives no general instruction for rolling percentile dice against it or assigning a difficulty to a particular barrier. The table can describe what the effect looks like; whether it gets the hero entirely through this barrier calls for a ruling. A calculator displaying fifty-five cannot make that decision for you.

Sometimes a roll is appropriate. In the optional six-sided pool rule, divide the numerical rating on the selected scale by six. The author illustrates three dice at 18 C1 and four at 24 C2. The high-roll example gives three dice to both an elite human at 18 C2 and a low-level mutant at 18 C1. The pool is the same size though the characters differ in magnitude. The text gives no general way to round a fractional die count. A rule for skills and combat asks for a high result, at least sixty-six percent. If your group reads that as a percentage of the maximum possible total, the first passing total on three dice is twelve. There are 216 equally likely ways to throw three fair dice, and just 81 give twelve or more. That is a 37.5 percent chance of success under this reading. The distinction affects a player's choice to take a risk. The text states the percentage threshold; the denominator in this calculation is an interpretation.

The game also shows someone trying to reach or maintain a specified character state. One worked target is eleven or less. Here a low total is desirable. The introductory combat discussion points towards this roll-under passage, and the passage itself distinguishes some creation comparisons from rolls during play. Use the local example when the situation matches. A separate example reaches a target of eight or less in print and seven or less if you recompute a conversion. If the dice show eight, tell the player which reading the table is using before declaring the outcome. Neither target sets a general difficulty for every feat on the sheet.

When someone pushes back

Two people can use camouflage and keen observation in the same scene. Possessing one ability and describing the other supplies a good reason for a contest. It does not establish whose rating to use, which way the dice should go, or who wins if both players succeed. Even two speeds need care. A mutant movement passage reads a C3 baseline directly as miles per hour. For someone at twenty C2 that is about forty-nine miles per hour, while the ordinary running rule gives ten. A GM can select a movement model for the race. That selection changes what happens before anyone reaches for the dice.

In a superhero confrontation the GM can often get one further number. A rule for mutant damage would give a character at twenty C2 DP four six-sided damage dice. Suppose their sum is fourteen. You now know the damage total. To tell the next player what has changed, you still need a target, an applicable defense, the way damage becomes an injury, and a consequence that persists. Different passages propose defensive values or compare life points across games. None of those facts on its own makes fourteen a loss to the target's Default Psyche. Choosing such a rule would be a substantive act of game design.

A continuing game needs somewhere to write what lasts. Some local effects already say something useful: a creator's former status may return when a lasting effect expires naturally. The death-and-rebirth rule even gives a waiting time for particular extraordinary beings. Those are conditions with quite specific subjects. For an injured courier returning next week, the material provides neither a general healing interval nor an award of experience for completing the adventure. Preserve the sheet and the end of the fiction. If the group creates a consequence or recovery rule, record it alongside the session. The separate experimental game in this edition shows what those choices cost.

What to keep in front of you

For a character, start with a labelled baseline, its origin, and the traits or powers this particular build grants. For an action, ask for a verb, an intended result and the information its rule consumes. For an outcome, say what the procedure actually gives back. A pace met, a new trait acquired, an effect rated and a die target passed are four different answers.
